How color codes work

Every color you see on a screen is made from three lights: red, green and blue. Turn all three up to full power and you get white; turn them all off and you get black. Mix red and green with no blue and you get yellow. Every other color — all sixteen million or so of them — is some in-between mixture. A color code is simply a compact way of writing down that mixture so a computer can repeat it exactly.

The three systems on this page describe the same mixture from different angles. HEX packs the red, green and blue amounts into six characters, which is why web designers love it: it is short enough to paste into CSS. RGB writes the same three amounts as ordinary numbers from 0 to 255, which makes it easy to read and to adjust one channel at a time with the sliders above. HSL describes a color the way people naturally think about it — by its hue (where it sits on the rainbow), its saturation (how vivid or gray it is) and its lightness (how close it is to white or black). If you want the full story, read our article HEX, RGB and HSL explained in plain English.

Here is a quick check you can do yourself with the tool: set the picker to pure red. The HEX reads #FF0000, the RGB reads rgb(255, 0, 0), and the HSL reads hsl(0, 100%, 50%). Three different writings, one identical color. Pure green is #00FF00 and hsl(120, 100%, 50%); pure blue is #0000FF and hsl(240, 100%, 50%). Once you see that pattern — hue 0 is red, 120 is green, 240 is blue — the color wheel stops being mysterious.

HEX vs RGB vs HSL: which should you use?

There is no single “best” format; there is only the format that suits the job in front of you. Use HEX when you need to store or share a color — in a style guide, a brand document, an email to a developer, or a design tool. It is the most compact and the most widely understood. Use RGB when you need transparency: the rgba() form adds a fourth number for opacity, which is how you create soft overlays and shadows that let the background show through. Use HSL when you are creating variations by hand, because its numbers map to human intentions. Need a lighter version of a button color for its hover state? Keep the hue and saturation, raise the lightness by ten points. Need a muted version for a background? Lower the saturation. With HEX, those same adjustments are guesswork.

A practical workflow many designers follow: choose colors in HSL because it is intuitive, store the final choices as HEX because it is portable, and switch to RGB only when transparency is involved. The converter above makes moving between the three effortless, so you never have to commit to one system for everything.

Choosing colors for a website

Start with the feeling, not the color. A law firm, a children's toy shop and a music festival should not share a palette, because color sets expectations before a single word is read. Write down three words for the mood of your site — calm and trustworthy, bold and energetic, warm and handmade — and let those words narrow the hue families worth exploring. Our article on what colors mean in design walks through the associations each major hue carries.

Then apply the 60-30-10 rule borrowed from interior design: roughly sixty percent of the page is a quiet neutral (white, off-white, light gray), thirty percent is your main brand color family, and ten percent is a contrasting accent reserved for the things you most want noticed — buttons, links, prices, calls to action. If the accent appears everywhere, it stops being an accent. Finally, check that your text is actually readable: dark text on a light background or light text on a dark background, with enough contrast between them. This is not only a matter of taste; it is an accessibility requirement covered in our guide to web accessibility and color contrast.

Test your palette on a real page, not just as swatches. Colors behave differently at different sizes: a vivid orange that looks exciting as a small button can feel exhausting as a full-page background, and a pale gray that looks elegant as a background can make body text strain the eyes. Use the shades and tints generator above to build a small scale for your main color — background tint, border tone, main shade, dark shade for text — and your design will look coherent almost automatically.

What colors mean

Color carries meaning whether we plan it or not. Blue suggests trust and stability, which is why banks and technology companies reach for it so often. Green is tied to nature, health and growth. Red creates urgency and appetite — it speeds the pulse a little — so it appears on sale banners and food brands, but large areas of it can feel aggressive. Yellow is cheerful and attention-grabbing yet tiring in bulk. Purple has long been linked with creativity and luxury. Orange feels friendly and affordable. Black signals sophistication; white signals simplicity and cleanliness; gray signals neutrality and professionalism.

These associations are tendencies, not laws, and culture shifts them — white means weddings in some countries and mourning in others. Treat meanings as a starting hypothesis, then check them against your actual audience. Common questions, briefly answered

Why does my color look different on my phone and my laptop?

Screens are calibrated differently, and brightness, night modes and ambient light all shift perception. The code is exact; the display is approximate. This is normal, and it is why professionals judge color by its numbers first and its appearance second.

What is the difference between a shade, a tint and a tone?

A shade is a color plus black, a tint is a color plus white, and a tone is a color plus gray. The generator above produces all three so you can compare them side by side.

Can I use these colors commercially?

Yes. A color value itself — a HEX code — is a fact, not a creative work, and you may use any code you find or create here in personal and commercial projects. (A specific shade can occasionally be trademarked as part of a brand identity, so avoid deliberately imitating a famous brand's exact presentation.)

HEX, RGB and HSL Explained in Plain English

A ColorPickerHub guide to the three color systems used on every screen.

Every color on every screen — this page, your phone, a television — is built from three lights: red, green and blue. That is the whole secret. The systems called HEX, RGB and HSL are just three different ways of writing down how much of each light to use, or, in HSL's case, a clever re-description of the same information in human terms. Once you understand that they all describe the same thing, converting between them stops being intimidating.

RGB: the raw ingredients

RGB is the most direct system. It lists three numbers, each from 0 to 255: how much red, how much green, how much blue. rgb(255, 0, 0) means full red, no green, no blue — pure red. rgb(0, 0, 0) is black, because all the lights are off. rgb(255, 255, 255) is white, because all three are at full power. rgb(128, 128, 128) is a mid gray, all three equal and halfway up.

Why 255? Because each channel is stored in one byte, and a byte holds 256 values, numbered 0 to 255. Three channels at 256 values each gives 256 × 256 × 256 = 16,777,216 possible colors — the “16.7 million colors” you see advertised on monitors.

HEX: the same numbers in a compact code

HEX (hexadecimal) writes the exact same three numbers in base 16 instead of base 10, then joins them into one six-character code. Base 16 uses the digits 0–9 plus the letters A–F, so each channel fits in exactly two characters: 0 becomes 00, 255 becomes FF, and 59 becomes 3B. Pure red, rgb(255, 0, 0), becomes #FF0000. The blue used as this site's starting example, rgb(59, 130, 246), becomes #3B82F6 — FF-style pairs 3B, 82, F6. The # symbol simply announces “a HEX color follows.”

You will also meet a short three-character form such as #F00. That is shorthand: each character is doubled, so #F00 means #FF0000. Browsers accept both. HEX became the web's favorite because it is short, unambiguous and easy to paste into CSS, HTML and design tools — try pasting any code into the picker on our homepage and watch the RGB sliders move to match.

HSL: color described the way people think

HSL rearranges the same information into hue, saturation and lightness. Hue is an angle on a color wheel, from 0 to 360: 0 is red, 60 is yellow, 120 is green, 180 is cyan, 240 is blue, 300 is magenta, and 360 wraps back to red. Saturation is a percentage: 100% is the pure, vivid hue; 0% is gray, with no trace of the hue at all. Lightness is also a percentage: 0% is black, 100% is white, and 50% is the hue at its most natural strength.

So pure red is hsl(0, 100%, 50%). Our example blue is hsl(217, 91%, 60%) — hue 217 sits between cyan-blue (210) and pure blue (240), it is highly saturated, and it is a little lighter than mid. The power of HSL is adjustment: want that blue softer for a background? Lower saturation to 45% and raise lightness to 88%. In HEX, that edit is a research project; in HSL, it is two obvious numbers.

Which one should you use, and when?

Store and share colors in HEX, because it is compact and universal. Adjust and build families of colors in HSL, because its numbers match your intentions. Use RGB when you need transparency — rgba(59, 130, 246, 0.5) is the example blue at half opacity, something neither plain HEX nor HSL expresses as clearly (though modern CSS adds alpha forms to all three). And when a printer asks for CMYK, know that it is a different system entirely, based on inks rather than light; screen colors converted to print often look duller, which is expected, not a mistake.

The best way to make all of this stick is ten minutes of play: open the ColorPickerHub picker, drag one slider at a time, and watch all three codes change together. Then read how to choose a palette to put the knowledge to work.

How to Choose a Color Palette for Your Website

A practical, step-by-step method that works even if you do not think of yourself as “good with color.”

Most bad website color is not caused by bad taste. It is caused by choosing colors one at a time, in isolation, with no system. A palette is a system: a small set of colors with assigned jobs, tested together. Follow the steps below and you will end up with something coherent, even if no individual choice is brilliant.

Step 1: Decide the feeling before the color

Write down three adjectives for your site: “calm, expert, friendly” for a clinic; “fast, bold, rebellious” for a skate shop. Colors are emotional shorthand — our guide to color meanings in design maps each hue family to the feelings it tends to trigger. Let the adjectives pick the neighborhood (blues and greens for calm; reds and oranges for energy) before you pick any specific shade.

Step 2: Choose one base color

Pick a single main color that carries your brand. Find it in the picker by exploring hues at a moderate saturation — very vivid colors are hard to live with in large amounts, and very gray ones can feel lifeless. A useful trick: if the color would look acceptable as a shirt you would wear all day, it is probably in a workable range.

Step 3: Build its family — shades, tints and tones

From the base color, generate darker shades for headings and hover states, lighter tints for backgrounds and panels, and grayish tones for borders and secondary elements. The homepage tool produces all of these from one click. A family built from a single hue always harmonizes with itself, which is why single-hue (“monochromatic”) schemes are the safest starting point for beginners.

Step 4: Add one accent — and ration it

An accent is a contrasting color reserved for action: buttons, links, the “buy” or “book” element. Colors opposite each other on the wheel (blue/orange, purple/yellow, red/green) contrast most strongly. Apply the 60-30-10 rule: about 60% neutral, 30% your main family, 10% accent. The accent's rarity is exactly what makes visitors' eyes go to it.

Step 5: Choose neutrals deliberately

Your text color, background and borders do more visual work than your brand color ever will. Pure black text (#000000) on pure white can feel harsh; a very dark gray such as #1C1917 on an off-white such as #FAF9F7 is softer while keeping strong contrast. Slightly warm or cool grays that lean toward your main hue make a design feel intentional rather than defaulted.

Step 6: Test like a visitor, not like a designer

Put the palette on a real page with real text. Check three things: body text is effortless to read (see the contrast rules in our accessibility guide), the main button is the first thing your eye finds, and nothing important is communicated by color alone — a red error message should also say, in words, what went wrong, for visitors who cannot distinguish red. Then view the page outdoors on a phone, where sunlight washes everything out; palettes that survive sunlight survive anything.

Finally, write your final codes down — HEX values in a small style note you keep with the site. Consistency over time is what turns “some colors” into “a brand.”

Color Meanings in Design: What Each Color Says About Your Brand

The associations each major hue carries, where it works, and where it backfires.

Visitors form an impression of a website in well under a second, and color does much of that work before a headline is even read. The meanings below are broad tendencies in Western audiences — culture, context and shade all modify them — but they are reliable enough to steer real decisions, especially when combined with a sensible palette-building method.

Blue — trust, calm, competence

Blue is the safest color in design and the most common choice for banks, insurers, software and healthcare. Light blues feel open and friendly; navy feels established and serious. The risk is blandness: because everyone trustworthy chooses blue, blue alone rarely makes a brand memorable. Pair it with a distinctive accent.

Green — nature, health, growth

Green signals the outdoors, freshness, money and “go.” It suits food, wellness, environment and finance brands. Bright lime greens feel youthful and digital; deep forest greens feel premium and calm. Avoid sickly yellow-greens for anything edible or medical — the association flips from fresh to unwell very quickly.

Red — urgency, appetite, passion

Red raises heart rates and demands attention. It sells sales, fast food and sports. Used as an accent — a button, a price, a deadline — it is powerful. Used as a background for long text it becomes tiring and can read as a warning. In interfaces, red also means “error” and “delete,” so never use it casually for ordinary actions.

Yellow and orange — warmth, optimism, value

Yellow is the most visible color and the happiest, but large areas of it strain the eyes and it is nearly impossible to read as text on white. Orange keeps much of the warmth with better manners: friendly, energetic, affordable. Both work well for children's products, food, travel and calls to action aimed at a young audience.

Purple — creativity, luxury, imagination

Historically the rarest dye and the color of royalty, purple still carries premium and creative associations. Deep purples suit beauty, luxury and entertainment; soft lavenders feel gentle and are common in wellness and baby products. Purple's weakness is legibility at small sizes — give purple text plenty of contrast.

Black, white and gray — the neutrals that speak

Black signals sophistication, power and formality — fashion and luxury brands build entire identities on it. White signals simplicity, honesty and cleanliness; it is also the canvas that makes other colors look brighter. Gray signals neutrality, balance and professionalism, but an all-gray design can feel cold or unfinished. Warm grays (with a hint of brown or yellow) feel human; cool grays (with a hint of blue) feel technical.

Use meanings as a compass, not a cage

A children's brand in navy and gray would feel wrong; a funeral service in lime green would feel worse. But within the right neighborhood, your exact shade is yours to choose — test candidates in the picker, build their shades and tints, and trust the combination of meaning plus readability. And remember that meaning never excuses inaccessibility: a beautiful low-contrast palette is still a broken one, as our contrast guide explains.

Web Accessibility and Color Contrast: A Practical Guide

How to keep your text readable for everyone — including visitors with low vision or color blindness — without giving up good design.

Roughly one in twelve men and one in two hundred women have some form of color vision deficiency, and many more people read screens with aging eyes, in bright sunlight, or on dim, cheap displays. Designing for contrast is not charity; it is designing for reality. The good news: the rules are simple numbers, and meeting them usually makes a design look sharper for everyone.

What a contrast ratio is

Contrast ratio compares the brightness of text to the brightness of its background, from 1:1 (identical colors — invisible) to 21:1 (black on white). The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) set the widely used thresholds: normal text should reach at least 4.5:1 (level AA), and large text — roughly 18pt and above, or 14pt bold — at least 3:1. The stricter AAA level asks for 7:1 and 4.5:1 respectively. If you remember only one number, remember 4.5:1 for body text.

Easy to read
Dark text #1C1917 on white — ratio about 17:1. Passes every level.
Hard to read
Light gray text #A8A29E on white — ratio about 2.4:1. Fails for body text.

The second box above is the single most common accessibility failure on the modern web: fashionable pale-gray text. It looks refined on a designer's calibrated monitor and disappears on everyone else's screens.

Fixing low contrast without ruining the design

You rarely need new colors — you need adjusted ones. Take your too-light text color into the ColorPickerHub picker and switch to its HSL values: keep the hue and saturation, and lower the lightness step by step until the color is visibly firm against its background. The same trick works in reverse for light text on dark backgrounds. Because HSL separates lightness from hue, your brand color's personality survives the fix.

Never let color be the only signal

Contrast is only half the story. If an error is shown only by turning a field red, a visitor with red-green color blindness may see nothing wrong at all. Always pair color with a second signal: an icon, a label, an underline on links (not just a color change), or plain words — “Error: this field is required.” Charts should label lines directly or use patterns as well as colors. Forms should never rely on “the green button” in their instructions; say “the Continue button.”

A five-minute contrast checklist

  1. Body text meets 4.5:1 against its background — including text over photos, where the lightest part of the photo is what counts.
  2. Headings and large text meet 3:1.
  3. Links inside paragraphs are identifiable without color alone — underline them.
  4. Buttons and form borders are distinguishable from the background at 3:1, so visitors can find the controls.
  5. Placeholder text in form fields is not your only label, and is not pale gray.
  6. Zoom the page to 200% and view it in bright light: if it is still comfortable, it is genuinely accessible.

Accessibility and beauty are not opponents. The crisp, confident designs people admire almost always have strong contrast built in. Build your palette with the method in our palette guide, then run this checklist over it — your visitors, all of them, will feel the difference.

Frequently asked questions

Is ColorPickerHub free to use?

Yes. The picker, the converter, the shades and tints generator and all articles are free, with no account and no sign-up. The tool runs in your browser.

What is a HEX color code?

A HEX code is a six-character representation of a color's red, green and blue values, written in base 16 and preceded by a # sign — for example #3B82F6. It is the most common way to specify color in CSS. Our article HEX, RGB and HSL explained covers it fully.

How do I convert HEX to RGB?

Split the code into three pairs — 3B, 82, F6 — and convert each pair from base 16 to decimal: 59, 130, 246, giving rgb(59, 130, 246). Or simply paste the HEX code into the picker and read the answer instantly.

What is the difference between a shade, a tint and a tone?

A shade mixes a color with black (darker), a tint mixes it with white (lighter), and a tone mixes it with gray (softer). The homepage tool generates all three ranges for any color you pick.

Why do colors look different when printed?

Screens emit light (RGB); printers apply ink (CMYK), which absorbs light. The printable range of colors is smaller, so vivid screen colors — especially bright greens and blues — print duller. This is a physical limit, not an error in your file.

What contrast ratio should my website text have?

At least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, per the WCAG AA standard. See our accessibility and contrast guide for practical fixes.

Can I use colors I find here in commercial work?

Yes. Color values are facts and may be used freely in personal and commercial projects. Avoid deliberately imitating a well-known brand's overall look, which can raise trademark issues — but a HEX code itself is yours to use.

Does the site store the colors I pick?
